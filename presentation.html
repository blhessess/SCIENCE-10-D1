Start with what you know

Where have you heard “acid”?

Food

Lemon juice and vinegar contain acids.

Our bodies

Stomach fluid is acidic and helps digestion.

Products

Some cleaners and batteries involve acidic or basic substances.

Turn and talk: Which examples have you encountered? What do you already think “acidic” means?

Acids

What is an acid?

In water, acids increase the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+). Many acids have a pH below 7.

Examples

Citric acid in citrus fruits; acetic acid in vinegar; hydrochloric acid in gastric juice.

Indicators

Blue litmus paper typically turns red in an acidic solution.

Never identify a substance by tasting it. Some acids are corrosive and hazardous.

Bases

What is a base?

Many bases increase hydroxide ions (OH−) in water. Basic (alkaline) solutions have a pH above 7.

Examples

Baking soda solution and some household cleaners are basic. (Products vary—check labels.)

Indicators

Red litmus paper typically turns blue in a basic solution.

Bases can also be corrosive. Never touch, mix, or test household chemicals in class.

The pH scale

A scale for acidity

0   acidic   ←   7 neutral   →   basic   14

pH is a scale related to hydrogen-ion concentration. Lower pH means more acidic; higher pH means more basic. The scale is logarithmic, so a one-unit change is significant.

Pure water is near pH 7 at room temperature. Real solutions can have different pH values; indicators give clues, not always an exact measurement.

When acids and bases react

Neutralization can make a salt

A typical acid–base neutralization produces water and an ionic compound called a salt.

acid + base → salt + water

Example: hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water.

HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O

This is a simplified example. The exact products depend on the reactants and conditions.

Salts are more than table salt

What do chemists mean by “salt”?

A salt is an ionic compound formed from positive and negative ions. Sodium chloride (table salt) is one familiar example; many other salts exist.

Everyday connections

Salts are found in foods, minerals, and many useful materials.

Key idea

“Salt” in chemistry does not mean only the seasoning in a salt shaker.

Think like a scientist

Quick check

  1. A solution has pH 3. Is it acidic, neutral, or basic?
  2. Which indicator color change suggests an acid with blue litmus?
  3. In a typical neutralization, what two kinds of products form?
  4. Why should we never taste an unknown substance?

Discuss: 1) Acidic. 2) Blue litmus turns red. 3) A salt and water. 4) Unknown substances may be harmful.
